What I’m reading: AI, bias and work

A system can work as designed and still distribute its benefits unevenly. These readings are a starting point for asking better questions about AI and work.

By Sarah Forrest · Updated November 22, 2026

01 / Who expects work to change?

Start with the World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025. Notice whose expectations are represented and which workers may be less visible. Compare an employer's stated need for new skills with the time and resources workers actually have to learn them.

02 / Exposure is not the same as displacement

Anthropic's March 2026 labor-market research combines measures of capability and observed usage to study AI exposure. Its early evidence does not establish that every exposed job will disappear. Separate what a tool can do, what people use it for and what employers change as a result. Those are different questions.

03 / Read a foundational bias paper

Bolukbasi and colleagues' 2016 paper, Man is to Computer Programmer as Woman is to Homemaker?, examines gender stereotypes in word embeddings and methods for reducing them. It concerns a particular representation and evaluation setting. It does not prove that a modern assistant is unbiased, or that a technical adjustment resolves every downstream harm.

Map one system

Take an AI-assisted hiring workflow. Draw the path from job description to screening, interview and decision. At each step, ask who supplies the data, who reviews the output and who can challenge an error. A change that saves time at screening could also change who gets considered.

Questions that connect things

Use these questions in a meeting, a design review or your own experiment. Systems thinking begins by following consequences beyond the first result.

  • Who benefits from this change?
  • Who takes on extra work or risk?
  • What information is missing?
  • What new problem could the solution create?
  • How would we notice an uneven outcome?
  • Can feedback from affected people improve the next version?
